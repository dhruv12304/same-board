Same Board, Different Game

In 2026, Britain's only all-women chess team won promotion to the top division of its national league. Then the rulebook told them to drop a woman and play a man. The rule was written to help women. So what is it actually saying about them?

First, a guess

If men and women played chess in equal numbers, how many of the world's top 100 players would you expect to be women?

Your guessPool-size model: about 50

The model assumes skill is spread the same way in both groups. Not every researcher thinks that explains everything, but it explains more than most people expect. The next section shows why.

A bigger group almost always has the best player

Bilalić and colleagues studied Germany's rating database, which had about 16 men for every woman. Their point is simple statistics: if two groups have the same spread of skill, the bigger group is far more likely to hold the very top spot. Try it.

Each dot is one simulated player. Both groups come from the same skill curve (average 1500, typical spread 300). The only difference is how many players each group has.

The same logic runs all the way down the rankings. Slide the women's share of players and watch the gap between each group's best players shrink.

Expected rating of the top 50 in each group, from a model of 120,000 players with identical skill curves. Bilalić et al. found this kind of model predicted about 96% of the real gap among the top 100. Knapp (2010) argued it explains less when the shape of the rating curve is modeled differently.

Pool size can explain who sits at the top. It can't explain why the pool is so small in the first place.

Why so few women play

What adults expect

Arnold and colleagues asked 286 parents and mentors about 654 young players. The adults were just as willing to spend time and money on girls as on boys. But they expected girls to peak lower, and the gap was widest among adults who believe chess success takes innate brilliance.

Where you grow up

At Candidate Master level, Vietnam has about 48 women for every 100 men. The United States has about 4. Across countries, a history of command economy was the strongest predictor of a smaller gap, even after accounting for wealth and gender equality. And in more gender-equal countries, a lower share of girls tends to play.

How women were pushed to the side

Chess wasn't always a men's game. The split came when it turned into an organized sport.

  1. For centuries

    Chess is seen as a pastime for both women and men.

  2. 1800s

    Chess becomes an organized sport, and its institutions push women out.

  3. 1927

    The first Women's World Championship gives women their own stage, a separate one.

  4. Today

    Separate titles and events remain, now alongside quotas.

Based on Stempień's sociological history of women in chess.

Against men, women play a little worse

Backus and colleagues measured how far each move strayed from a chess engine's best move, across about 29,000 games. Women made about 11% more errors against men than against women. Men played the same against either. The effect works out to roughly a 25-point rating handicap.

Why it happens is still argued. A 2018 study of 5.5 million games found women did better than their ratings predicted against men. Two 2020 commentaries pointed out that the women in that data were much younger, and young players are often better than their ratings show. Once both players' ages were accounted for, the result flipped.

So do women need their own spaces? A lab study of new video-game players offers a clue. In women-only tournaments, women judged their skill accurately and cared about winning. In mixed ones, they underrated themselves. The researcher's conclusion was that women-only spaces should build skill, "not segregate permanently."

A door

Women-only play as a starting point. It builds skill and confidence, then leads into open competition.

A lower rung

Women-only play as the destination. It becomes permanent and marks women's chess as second tier.

The team that won its way out of eligibility

She Plays To Win Lionesses A, Britain's only all-women team, earned promotion to Division 1 of the Four Nations Chess League. Step through what the rulebook did next.

  1. They win promotion

    The all-women team plays its way out of Division 2.

  2. Division 2 had allowed them Rule 1.2

    Teams there need two of three groups: adult men, adult women, juniors. Women plus a junior qualifies.

  3. Division 1 requires both sexes Rule 1.2

    Every top-division team must include male and female players. One of the women has to make way for a man.

  4. Even ratings bend Rule 8.5

    To meet the gender requirement, one player may be lower rated than board order normally allows.

"We got to the top proving everyone wrong, and now this undermines that."
Emily Maton, Lionesses player
"There is no evidence that the rule as it stands discourages female participation in chess."
Mike Truran, 4NCL

The league says its top division has required mixed teams since it was founded in 1994, and a 2025/26 survey of captains found "no significant appetite" to change that. But the rule assumes women will always be scarce. The Lionesses exist to change exactly that.

Two ladders, one board

FIDE awards open titles that anyone can earn, and a parallel set for women only. Each women's title starts 200 points below the open title with the same name. A Woman Grandmaster's minimum rating equals a FIDE Master's.

If the gap at the top is mostly about how many women play, a permanently lower women's title records a participation gap as if it were an ability gap. It echoes the lower ceiling Arnold's adults set for girls.

Grade the rules

A 2023 review of the research named the field's "main weakness": "the lack of experimental studies." Researchers keep explaining the gap. Almost nobody tests whether the rules built to close it work. Until they do, here are three questions to ask of any rule, drawn from the research above.

Most findings here are correlational: they show patterns, not proof of cause. The Lionesses case is one team in one league. The simulations are simple models built from published figures, not new data.
